# Module 3 — Curiosity-gap & retrieval-practice science

**Date:** 2026-11-22 · **Domains:** D11 (curiosity-gap and retrieval-practice science)
**Status:** DONE as a learning module. **Level after this module: D11 LEARNING — not TESTED.**
No account data exists yet and no video of ours has retention or memory data, so nothing
here is proven about *our* format. This module changes what the factory builds next
(question-design rules, reveal timing, post-reveal placement, test designs), and its
claims stay labelled until our own curves and comment data exist.

**How to read the labels:** [OFFICIAL] = TikTok's own pages or publications.
[ACADEMIC] = peer-reviewed or published research with data. [PRACTITIONER — with data] =
a practitioner analysis that shows its numbers, sample and method, and can be checked.
[PRACTITIONER — claim] = advice with no data shown. [UNPROVEN] = a number or rule with no
source the factory could verify. [INFERENCE] = the factory's own reasoning from the above,
flagged as such. A claim's label is part of the claim.

**The one-sentence version:** a quiz question works because it opens a felt gap in what
the viewer knows, the wait before the answer is where the memory benefit is built, and
the answer only pays off if the viewer finds it interesting — but the curiosity a
question creates *narrows* attention, so anything unrelated placed right after a reveal
is remembered worse, not better. The factory's job is to open gaps a viewer can almost
close, hold them long enough for a real guess, close them cleanly, and put nothing that
must be remembered in the shadow of a reveal.

## 1. The theory: curiosity is a felt gap, and the gap has a size

### 1.1 Loewenstein's information-gap theory

[ACADEMIC] Loewenstein (1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1): 75–98) proposed that
curiosity is a cognitively induced state of deprivation: it arises when attention fixes
on a gap between what a person knows and what they want to know, and its intensity
depends on the size and salience of that gap. Two consequences matter for question
design. First, curiosity requires prior knowledge: a person who knows nothing about a
topic perceives no gap in it, so a question about something wholly unfamiliar cannot
make them curious — it can only confuse them. Second, curiosity is topic-specific and
situational: it is a property of the encounter (the question, the framing), not only of
the person. Note the scope: this is a theory paper, a reinterpretation of the earlier
literature, not an experiment — Loewenstein offers no new data of his own. The theory
is the frame; the experiments below are the evidence.

### 1.2 The inverted U: curiosity peaks at "almost knowing"

[ACADEMIC] Kang et al. (2009, *Psychological Science* 20(8): 963–973) found that
curiosity follows an inverted-U over confidence: people were least curious when they had
no idea at all and least curious when they were already highly confident they knew the
answer; curiosity peaked in the middle, where someone had a partial sense of the answer
but real uncertainty remained. [ACADEMIC] Metcalfe's Region of Proximal Learning (RPL)
framework (Metcalfe, 2020, *Current Opinion in Behavioral Science* 35: 40–47; building
on Metcalfe, 2002 and Metcalfe & Kornell, 2003) says the same thing in learning terms:
people are most curious and engaged, and learn best, when the material feels "almost
known" — neither so trivial that boredom follows nor so difficult and far removed from
their schemata that they cannot understand its significance. The RPL is different for
different people, as a function of their expertise in the domain. [ACADEMIC] A 2025
study (Metacognition and Learning 20:1) using a blurred-picture paradigm found the same
shape again: curiosity was particularly heightened when Knowledge Confidence was at low
or medium levels, and learning was best predicted by a learner's metacognitive appraisal
of being on the verge of knowing — an effect independent of curiosity itself.

[INFERENCE] For the factory, the inverted U is the gap-sizing rule in its theoretical
form: a question must be *recognisable* enough that the viewer feels they almost know
it, and *uncertain* enough that they do not. A question nobody can approach (no
foothold, no familiar element) opens no gap; a question everyone answers instantly
closes the gap before curiosity can form. The factory cannot measure a viewer's
confidence directly, so gap size must be calibrated by proxy — see §9(a).

### 1.3 Curiosity is a drive with a price: it narrows attention

[ACADEMIC] The same arousal that makes curiosity powerful also makes it narrow.
Keller et al. (2024, §3) interpret their interference finding through Mather's
arousal-biased competition model: arousal enhances memory for information that matches
the person's goal state and impairs memory for information that does not. A trivia
question is a goal state — resolve *this* uncertainty — and anything that does not serve
that goal is, in the moment, competition. This is the theoretical reason the post-reveal
dead zone (§3) exists, and why it cannot be wished away by making the post-reveal
material "interesting": interest in the *question* is what narrows the attention, not
boredom with the material after it.

## 2. Curiosity and memory: what the gap does while it is open

### 2.1 Gruber et al. 2014 — the anticipatory period builds the memory

[ACADEMIC] Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014, *Neuron* 84(2): 486–496). Nineteen
participants rated their curiosity about trivia questions, then waited 14 seconds —
during which an unrelated photograph of a face was shown — before seeing the answer.
In both immediate and one-day-delayed memory tests, participants remembered the answers
to high-curiosity questions better than low-curiosity ones (70.6% vs 54.1% recall), and
also remembered the incidental faces shown during high-curiosity waits better. fMRI
showed enhanced activity in the midbrain (ventral tegmental area) and nucleus accumbens
during high-curiosity states, and individual differences in the memory benefit for
incidental material were supported by anticipatory activity in the midbrain and
hippocampus and by functional connectivity between them. The authors' conclusion: the
*anticipatory* period — the wait before the answer — is where the memory benefit is
built, through dopaminergic modulation of the hippocampus.

Three cautions, all from the paper itself. First, the sample is small (19 participants)
and the setting is a scanner, not a feed. Second, the incidental material that benefited
was simple visual stimuli (faces), presented *during the wait*, not complex verbal
material presented after the answer — which is exactly the boundary Keller et al. (2024)
later found (§3). Third, the benefit is for material the viewer is curious *about* and
for simple material that happens to be present during the wait; it is not a general
claim that curiosity improves everything nearby.

### 2.2 Kang et al. 2009 — curiosity, reward circuitry, and surprising answers

[ACADEMIC] Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang & Camerer (2009,
*Psychological Science* 20(8): 963–973). Curiosity ratings while reading trivia
questions correlated with activity in the caudate nucleus, a region involved in
anticipated reward. In a behavioural follow-up, participants spent more of scarce
resources — limited tokens, or waiting time — to learn answers they were more curious
about, and after an 11- to 16-day retention period they recalled the answers to
high-curiosity questions better. Two findings matter most for the factory. First,
curiosity increased activity in memory areas when participants guessed *incorrectly*,
and higher curiosity predicted better recall of *surprising* answers 1–2 weeks later:
the answer that contradicts a guess is remembered better than the answer that confirms
it. Second, curiosity followed the inverted-U over confidence described in §1.2.

[INFERENCE] The "surprising answer" finding is the scientific basis for the factory's
common-mistake question type (already in the hook library, M2 §3.3): a question whose
obvious answer is wrong does double work — it opens a gap, and the reveal lands on a
surprise that the brain is primed to keep. But the surprise must be *understood* as
correct (see §4.3 on hypercorrection and false feedback): a surprising answer the viewer
cannot verify is a confusion, not a memory.

## 3. The boundary: Keller et al. 2024 — curiosity interferes with what follows

This is the most important paper in the module, because it overturns the simple reading
of Gruber and it directly governs what the factory may place after a reveal.

### 3.1 What they did

[ACADEMIC] Keller, Salvi, Leiker, Gruber & Dunsmoor (2024, *npj Science of Learning*,
9: 23; PMC10948872). 250 participants enrolled online (CloudResearch/MTurk), 239
remained after exclusions (US adults, 18–50, English first language). They adapted
Gruber's trivia paradigm, but the incidental material was not faces — it was 50
*scholastic facts* (science, history, English), unrelated to the trivia questions. Three
versions of the task varied when the fact appeared:

- **Early Anticipation:** the fact appeared immediately after the participant rated
  their curiosity for the trivia question, before the answer.
- **Late Anticipation:** the same, but with a 4-second delay (a slightly prolonged
  curiosity state) before the fact.
- **Post Satisfaction:** the fact appeared *immediately following the answer* to the
  trivia question — the closest analogue to the factory's post-reveal beat.

Participants were told to pay close attention to the facts. Memory was then tested with
a surprise multiple-choice "pop quiz" on the scholastic facts.

### 3.2 What they found — interference in all three versions

[ACADEMIC] In **all three versions**, memory was significantly *worse* for scholastic
facts paired with high-curiosity trivia questions than for facts paired with low-curiosity
questions:

- Early Anticipation: t(94) = −2.940, p = 0.004, 95% CI [−0.060, −0.012]
- Late Anticipation: t(47) = −2.478, p = 0.017, 95% CI [−0.086, −0.009]
- Post Satisfaction: t(95) = −3.467, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.074, −0.020]

The overall ANOVA showed a main effect of curiosity category (F(1, 236) = 25.05,
p < 0.001, η²G = 0.009 — a small effect) and no interaction with task version
(p = 0.788): the interference did not depend on *when* the fact appeared. A mixed model
on trial-level ratings confirmed it (OR = 0.93 per curiosity point, 95% CI [0.89, 0.98],
p = 0.004). The authors' conclusion, in their words: target information that generates
a high-curiosity state "interferes with encoding complex and unrelated scholastic facts
presented close in time."

[ACADEMIC] The effect is small in absolute terms — the confidence intervals are on the
order of 1–9 percentage points of memory accuracy — but it is consistent across three
independent task versions and 239 participants, and it runs in the *opposite* direction
to the Gruber spillover finding. The difference, the authors argue, is the nature of the
incidental material: simple visual stimuli (faces) benefit from a curiosity state,
because they are distinct enough to "pop out" and do not compete for the same processing
resources; complex scholastic facts compete with the trivia question for the same
epistemic processing, and lose. Both the fact and the question are "knowledge the
participant is voluntarily motivated to attend to" — the more interesting question
overshadows the less interesting fact.

### 3.3 Limits of the study — stated by the authors, and they matter

[ACADEMIC] The paper's own limitations, which the factory must carry forward:

- It was conducted online during the COVID-19 pandemic, and online research is less
  controlled than laboratory research (though attention checks were used).
- Participants were **not tested on the trivia answers themselves**, so the study does
  not replicate Gruber's benefit for the target material — it only shows the cost to the
  incidental material. The factory cannot read this paper as saying curiosity is bad for
  the answer; it says curiosity is bad for *other* material nearby.
- There was **no incentive to remember the scholastic facts** (no test was expected),
  unlike a classroom where students know the material will be tested. A viewer who is
  *told* something will matter may encode it differently — but a TikTok viewer is never
  told that, and a CTA is not a test.
- Memory was tested **immediately** after encoding. The longest delay used to test
  curiosity spillover in the literature so far is 24 hours; whether the interference
  persists after consolidation is unknown. The authors raise behavioural tagging as a
  possible mechanism by which a strong curiosity event could *retroactively* bolster
  weak memories formed nearby — but only after consolidation, and only as a prediction
  for future work, not a finding.
- The authors also note the phasic/tonic distinction: their design used trial-by-trial
  spikes of curiosity. A sustained (tonic) curiosity state built over several minutes
  might behave differently — and might, after a delay, retain the neuromodulatory benefit
  without the overshadowing. This is speculation in the discussion, not evidence.

[INFERENCE] The transfer to the feed is doubly indirect: the lab measures *memory for
facts* in people sitting at a computer doing a task, and the factory needs *attention
and retention* in people scrolling a feed. Keller et al. do not show that viewers swipe
away after a reveal, or that a CTA after a reveal fails. What they show is narrower and
more useful: in the seconds around a high-curiosity question and its answer, the viewer's
encoding resources are captured by the question, and unrelated material placed there is
remembered worse. The factory's translation of that into feed behaviour — "don't put
anything valuable there" — is an inference, and it is tested by H-M3-2 (§10), not proven
by this paper.

## 4. What makes an answer stick: post-answer interest, confidence, and surprise

### 4.1 Fastrich et al. 2018 — the answer matters more than the question

[ACADEMIC] Fastrich, Kerr, Castel & Murayama (2018, *Motivation Science* 4(3):
227–250). A normative database of 244 trivia questions, rated by N = 1,498
participants. Participants read a question, guessed the answer, rated their confidence
and their interest in learning the true answer, saw the answer, rated their interest
*in the answer*, and were memory-tested one week later. The headline result: the
positive relationship between pre-answer interest and memory was **fully mediated by
post-answer interest** — that is, being interested in the question only helped memory
insofar as it produced interest in the answer once it was seen. Confidence had both a
direct effect on memory and an effect mediated through interest. High-confidence errors
led to increased memory performance, partly explained by increased interest in the
answers.

[INFERENCE] This is the most operationally important finding in the module, and it
reframes the factory's job. The question's job is to create interest in the *answer*;
the answer's job is to repay it. A question that builds strong curiosity and then
delivers a flat, confusing, or anticlimactic answer wastes the curiosity it built —
because the memory benefit runs through post-answer interest, not around it. The reveal
is not a formality at the end of a gap; it is the moment the memory is actually made.
Concretely: the answer must be *understandable in the second it lands* (clear wording,
no jargon, no ambiguity about which option was right), and it should carry a brief
reason why it is true — the "why" is what turns an answer into something interesting
rather than a bare fact. A reveal that needs re-reading or re-listening to be understood
is a failed reveal, whatever the question was.

### 4.2 The hypercorrection effect — being confidently wrong is a memory opportunity

[ACADEMIC] Butterfield & Metcalfe (2006, *Metacognition and Learning* 1: 69–84).
People correct errors they committed with *high* confidence more easily than errors
they committed with low confidence, provided the correct answer is given as feedback.
The tested explanation: after a high-confidence error, the correct answer is surprising
and captures attention — in both experiments, a concurrent tone-detection task was
selectively impaired during feedback to high-confidence errors, and the impairment was
negatively related to final performance, meaning the attention not spent on the tone
was spent on the feedback. [ACADEMIC] Metcalfe (2019, *Cognitive Research: Principles
and Implications* 4: 4) confirmed in five experiments that participants hypercorrect
when they have made a mistake — they remember the correct feedback to high-confidence
errors better than to low-confidence errors — and showed the same pattern for *false*
feedback given after an error: when people are wrong initially, they hyper-encode
whatever feedback follows, true or false.

[INFERENCE] Two design consequences. First, the factory should deliberately include
questions where the confident, obvious answer is wrong — these are the questions whose
reveals are most memorable, because the viewer's own confidence is doing the work. The
hook library's "Common Mistake" pattern (M2 §3.3) is therefore not just a hook device;
it is the format's strongest memory device. Second, the Metcalfe 2019 warning makes
fact-checking load-bearing in a new way: if our answer is wrong, the viewers most
likely to remember it are exactly the ones who were most confidently wrong — and they
will remember the *wrong* answer. A fact error in a quiz video is not a small blemish;
it is a memorable falsehood planted in the viewers who engaged hardest. This links D11
directly to D9 (fact-checking): every answer must be verified before it is rendered,
and the verification must be at least as careful for the "obvious" answers as for the
obscure ones, because the obvious ones are where confident errors live.

### 4.3 Surprising vs expected answers — the synthesis

[ACADEMIC] Three independent lines converge: Kang et al. (2009) — curiosity enhances
memory for *surprising* answers 1–2 weeks later; Butterfield & Metcalfe (2006) — the
surprise of a high-confidence error captures attention and drives correction; Fastrich
et al. (2018) — high-confidence errors lead to better memory, partly through increased
post-answer interest. The common mechanism is not "surprise" in the abstract: it is a
violated expectation that the viewer *notices* and can *resolve*. A surprising answer
that the viewer cannot make sense of ("how can that be true?") leaves the expectation
violated without resolution, and the Fastrich mediation says the memory benefit then
has nowhere to go — post-answer interest collapses if the answer feels arbitrary.

[INFERENCE] The factory's reveal therefore has three jobs, in order: (1) state the
answer unambiguously; (2) show why the viewer's likely guess was wrong, in one short
sentence; (3) give the one fact that makes the true answer feel inevitable rather than
arbitrary. Job (2) is the surprise; job (3) is the resolution. A reveal that does only
(1) is a dead end; a reveal that does (1) and (2) without (3) is a trick. The one-line
"why" is *part of the reveal*, not an unrelated fact — which is why it is exempt from
the post-reveal rule in §8 (it is target material, not incidental material).

## 5. Retrieval practice: the viewer answering in their head is doing the learning

### 5.1 Roediger & Karpicke 2006 — testing beats restudying, even without feedback

[ACADEMIC] Roediger & Karpicke (2006, *Psychological Science* 17(3): 249–255).
Students learned short science passages, then either restudied them or took free-recall
tests on them, with no feedback. At five minutes, restudying was ahead (81% vs 75%
recalled in Experiment 1). At two days the order flipped, and after one week the
testing group remembered 56% against 42% for restudying (Experiment 1); in Experiment
2, students who read once and recalled three times remembered 61% a week later, against
40% for students who read four times — despite having read the passage only 3.4 times
on average, against 14.2 times for the rereaders. The students who only reread were
also the *most confident* they would remember. The testing effect held without any
feedback at all: the act of retrieving, not the correction, did the work.

### 5.2 Karpicke & Roediger 2008 — the exact numbers, and the dropout finding

[ACADEMIC] Karpicke & Roediger (2008, *Science* 319: 966–968). College students learned
40 Swahili–English word pairs. Once a pair was recalled correctly once, it was either
kept in study and test (ST), dropped from study but kept in test (SNT), dropped from
test but kept in study (STN), or dropped from both (SNTN). All four conditions reached
near-perfect performance by the end of learning, and all students predicted they would
recall about 50% a week later. Actual recall a week later: the two conditions that kept
*testing* recalled about 80%; the two that dropped testing recalled just 36% (STN) and
33% (SNTN) — even though the STN group had repeatedly *studied* every pair in every
study period. Combining the testing conditions against the dropped-testing conditions,
repeated retrieval increased final recall by 4 standard deviations (d = 4.03). Repeated
studying after one successful recall produced no measurable learning a week later.

[INFERENCE] The dropout finding is the one with the sharpest implication for the
factory's return loop. In a quiz video, the "dropout" analogue is a question the viewer
answered correctly once and never sees again — and the lab says that knowledge decays
to a third within a week. The factory's format should therefore *revisit* material:
a question, a fact, or a topic from an earlier video returning in a later one is not
repetition for its own sake; it is the retrieval that keeps the first encounter alive.
This is D12's territory (the return loop) as much as D11's, and it is why the module
queues a "returning question" hypothesis (H-M3-4) rather than treating each video as a
closed unit.

### 5.3 Adesope et al. 2017 — how big the effect is, across the field

[ACADEMIC] Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, *Review of Educational Research*
87(3): 659–701). A meta-analysis of practice testing: 272 effect sizes from 118
articles, 15,427 participants. Practice tests beat restudying by a weighted mean
Hedges' g = 0.51, and beat filler or no activity by g = 0.93 (fixed-effects overall
g = 0.61; random-effects g = 0.70). The authors note that g = 0.51 against restudying
is "arguably the most accurate indicator" of the benefit for everyday study habits,
because restudying is what people actually do instead. Two moderations worth recording:
the effect was *larger* when practice tests were used only once (g = 0.70) than when
used twice or more (g = 0.51) in the included studies — a counterintuitive pattern the
paper reports without a settled explanation — and the feedback moderation (g = 0.63 with
feedback vs 0.60 without) was not a significant difference, consistent with Roediger &
Karpicke (2006): feedback is not what makes retrieval work, though it is what makes
the factory's reveal satisfying.

[INFERENCE] The meta-analysis confirms the testing effect is not a one-lab artefact:
it is one of the most replicated findings in learning science, with a medium-to-large
effect against the realistic alternative (restudying). But every study in it is a study
of *deliberate learners* — students who chose to study, in a classroom or a lab. A
TikTok viewer did not choose to study; they chose to be entertained for 90 seconds.
The factory cannot import the effect size. What it can import is the mechanism: a
viewer who tries to answer before the reveal is doing retrieval practice, and a viewer
who only watches answers being given is doing restudying. The format's value proposition
— "you will actually remember these" — rests on the viewer's *attempt*, and the attempt
is what the think time exists for. If the think time is too short for a guess to form,
the video degrades from a retrieval exercise into a lecture with pauses.

## 6. Desirable difficulty: the right kind of hard

[ACADEMIC] Bjork (1994) coined "desirable difficulties": conditions of learning that
slow performance during training but enhance long-term retention and transfer — spacing,
interleaving, varying practice, and testing instead of restudying (Bjork & Bjork, 2011).
The framework's central distinction is between *performance* (what a learner can do
right now) and *learning* (what remains after a delay): conditions that maximise
performance often undermine learning, and vice versa. The framework also states its own
limits: a difficulty is only desirable if the task can still be accomplished — too
difficult a task dissuades the learner and prevents full processing — and if the
processing at encoding matches the processing at retrieval. [PRACTITIONER — claim]
Secondary summaries of Bjork's work add three practical guidelines (encoding and
retrieval processing must match; the task must be accomplishable; difficulty must be
productive, not mere friction) — these are reported from secondary sources, not read in
the primary text, and are recorded as such.

[INFERENCE] The factory's version of a desirable difficulty is a question that is hard
*in the right place*: hard to answer from memory, but easy to understand, easy to guess
at, and easy to verify once revealed. Difficulty that comes from unclear wording, an
obscure topic with no foothold, or a reveal that needs decoding is not desirable — it
is friction, and on a feed it is a swipe. The operational test is simple: if a viewer
who gets the question wrong can still say "ah, I see" within two seconds of the reveal,
the difficulty was desirable; if they say "what?", it was not.

## 7. What the lab does NOT tell us — the transfer gaps

Every finding above was produced in a laboratory or a classroom, with volunteers,
deliberate attention, and a memory test at the end. None of it was produced on a feed.
The gaps, stated plainly:

1. **Attention is not retention, and retention is not memory.** The lab measures whether
   a participant remembers an answer a day or a week later. The factory measures whether
   a viewer is still watching at second 45. Curiosity may hold attention without
   producing memory, and may produce memory without holding attention. No study in this
   module measures both in the same design, and no study measures swipe behaviour at
   all. Lab-to-feed transfer is an [INFERENCE] at every point in §9.
2. **No optimal hold time exists in the literature.** Gruber used a 14-second wait in a
   scanner; Keller used immediate presentation and a 4-second delay; neither study varies
   hold time as an independent variable against a retention outcome. The factory's 5 s vs
   10 s think-time question (M2 H3) has no literature answer. It is answered by our own
   curves.
3. **No study tests quiz videos.** The materials are trivia questions on paper, word
   pairs, and prose passages. Nothing in the literature tests a 90-second vertical video
   with a voiceover, captions, a countdown, and a reveal. Format, pacing, and production
   are all untested variables.
4. **Curiosity is measured per question, not per video.** The lab asks "how curious are
   you about this answer?" one question at a time. A 90-second video asks a viewer to
   sustain curiosity across 5–8 questions, with the previous answer still in mind. The
   Keller interference finding suggests each high-curiosity question may *cost* the next
   beat some encoding resource — but whether curiosity accumulates, fatigues, or resets
   across a round is [UNPROVEN].
5. **The Zeigarnik effect was not re-verified.** M2 §4.2 invoked it as the usual name
   for an unanswered question holding attention. This module did not check the primary
   literature on it, and it is not used as evidence here. It remains a named hypothesis,
   not a finding.

## 8. Verdict on M2 rule 6 — CONFIRMED, and refined in two directions

M2 rule 6 (provisional, [INFERENCE from Scan 1]): *"Nothing valuable immediately
after a reveal — the beat after a reveal is the next question's hook, not a fact, CTA,
or branding."*

**Verdict: CONFIRMED as a rule, and REFINED.** Keller et al. (2024) is the direct
evidence M2 was waiting for, and its Post Satisfaction version is the closest possible
analogue to the factory's post-reveal beat: material presented immediately after the
answer, following a high-curiosity question, was remembered worse — significantly, in
all three task versions, with no interaction by timing. The rule's core — do not place
material that must be remembered or acted on immediately after a reveal — is now
[ACADEMIC], not inference.

Two refinements, both with reasons:

**Refinement 1 — the rule is about *unrelated* material that must be encoded, not
about all material.** Keller's incidental facts were *unrelated* to the trivia
questions. A brief explanation of *why the answer is true* is not unrelated material:
it is part of the target, and Fastrich (§4.1) says post-answer interest is what carries
the memory benefit — the "why" is what makes the answer interesting. So the rule
becomes: *nothing unrelated that must be remembered or acted on immediately after a
reveal; a one-sentence "why" that belongs to the answer just given is part of the
reveal, not a violation of it.* A fact about a different topic, a CTA ("follow for part
2"), a brand message, and a score recap are all unrelated material and stay out of the
post-reveal beat.

**Refinement 2 — the next question's hook is the right beat, but for a different
reason than M2 gave.** M2's reasoning was that the next hook avoids the dead zone.
The refined reasoning: the next question's hook does not *need* the dead zone to be
clear, because it does not ask the viewer to encode anything — it asks them to feel a
new gap. The dead zone is a failure of *encoding*, not of *attention*: the viewer's
attention is captured by the previous question's resolution, and a new gap re-captures
it in the same direction. But this also means the next hook must be a *real* gap, not a
label: "Question 4" is not a hook; the question itself, asked in a way that makes the
viewer feel they almost know it, is. If the next beat is only a number and a title, the
viewer is left in the dead zone with nothing to attend to — which is the worst of both
worlds.

**The reveal itself gets a clean moment.** A corollary the module adds: the answer
should be displayed and spoken cleanly for a short beat (1–2 seconds) before the next
hook begins. Rushing from the answer into the next question truncates the post-answer
interest that Fastrich shows is doing the memory work, and it reads as though the
answer did not matter. The reveal is not a transition; it is the payoff. The beat
order is therefore: question → think time → reveal (answer + one-line why, clean, 1–2 s)
→ next question's hook. Nothing else enters that sequence.

**What the rule does not cover.** Keller et al. measured memory, not swiping. Whether
a CTA after a reveal also fails *behaviourally* (fewer follows, fewer comments) is
[UNPROVEN] until the factory tests it. The rule is adopted on the memory evidence plus
the inference that a viewer whose encoding is impaired is unlikely to act on what they
did not encode — and H-M3-2 (§10) is the test that would overturn it.

## 9. Application: what this changes in the 90-second format

### (a) Gap sizing — calibrating difficulty so the gap is feelable

[INFERENCE from §1.2, §2.2, §6] The target is the middle of the inverted U: the viewer
recognises the topic, feels they almost know the answer, and is not sure. Operationally,
the factory calibrates this by proxy, since it cannot ask a viewer their confidence:

- **The foothold test.** Every question must contain something the viewer already
  knows: a familiar name, a recognisable situation, a word they have heard. A question
  with no foothold ("What is the capital of a country you have never heard of?") opens
  no gap; it opens a blank. If the question cannot be rephrased to include a foothold,
  it is dropped.
- **The guess test.** A question is correctly sized if a viewer can form *a* guess in
  the think time — even a wrong one. A guess is the retrieval attempt (§5), and a wrong
  guess is the raw material for hypercorrection (§4.2). If the question is so hard that
  no guess forms, the think time is dead time and the reveal lands on nothing.
- **The mix rule.** Within a round, difficulty rises: an easy opener (a gap that closes
  fast, a small win, confidence built), medium questions in the middle, the hardest
  question last (the biggest gap, the strongest curiosity, the most memorable reveal).
  The opener exists to teach the viewer that they *can* play; the closer exists to make
  them remember the video. A round that opens with its hardest question risks the viewer
  concluding they cannot play at all.
- **The common-mistake slot.** At least one question per round should be a
  common-mistake question: the obvious answer is wrong. This is the format's strongest
  memory device (§4.2), and it is also the question most likely to be commented on
  ("I said X!"), which is the factory's win metric.
- **Calibration by evidence, not by feel.** The factory's own proxy for gap size is
  comment behaviour: a question that draws "I knew it!" and "I had no idea" in the same
  thread is correctly sized; a question that draws only silence or only "too easy" is
  not. Question difficulty ratings are recorded per question in the question bank, and
  revised from comment evidence — never from the author's feeling about how hard a
  question is.

### (b) Reveal timing — how long to hold the gap, and what the reveal must do

[ACADEMIC] The anticipatory period is where the memory benefit is built (Gruber 2014:
activity during the 14-second wait predicted later memory). [INFERENCE] But the factory
is not a scanner: on a feed, a hold that is too long is a swipe, and M2's retention
evidence says the first three seconds decide almost everything and lulls must be trimmed.
The resolution is not a number from the literature — there is none (§7.2) — it is a
design principle plus a test:

- **The hold must be long enough for a guess to form.** A guess is a retrieval attempt,
  and without it the video is a lecture (§5.3). The current 5 s vs 10 s test (M2 H3)
  is the instrument that finds the number; this module adds the *criterion* for reading
  it: the right hold is the shortest hold at which viewers report (in comments) having
  guessed. If viewers comment "I said Paris" under a 5-second hold, 5 seconds was
  enough; if the comments are only "too fast", it was not.
- **The hold must be active.** M2 rule 4 stands: every second of think time shows
  change (countdown, progress, elimination). A hold with nothing changing is a lull, and
  a lull is a swipe — whatever the curiosity literature says about waiting.
- **The reveal has three jobs** (§4.3): state the answer unambiguously; show why the
  likely guess was wrong, in one short sentence; give the one fact that makes the true
  answer feel inevitable. Then a clean 1–2 second beat on the answer before the next
  hook (§8). A reveal that fails job (1) — ambiguous, jargon, unclear which option was
  right — wastes the curiosity the question built, because post-answer interest cannot
  attach to an answer the viewer did not understand.
- **Riddles are a different hold.** A riddle's gap is not "do I know this fact?" but
  "can I solve this?" — the think time is problem-solving time, not recall time. The
  same hold-length test applies, but the criterion differs: the viewer needs time to
  *work*, not just to guess. The trivia and riddle hold times should be tested
  separately, not averaged.

### (c) What may and may not go immediately after a reveal

Decided in §8 (verdict: rule 6 confirmed and refined). In operational form:

- **May:** the answer itself, spoken and on screen, cleanly; one sentence of "why"
  that belongs to that answer; then the next question's hook (the question itself, asked
  so the viewer feels the new gap).
- **May not:** an unrelated fact, a CTA (follow, like, comment ask, "part 2"), a brand
  message, a score recap, a sponsor-style line, or any other material the viewer would
  need to remember or act on. These are not banned from the video — they are banned
  from the post-reveal beat. A CTA belongs where a gap is *not* open: the end of the
  video, after the final payoff, or before the first question, where no curiosity state
  exists to interfere with it. Both placements are hypotheses (H-M3-2), not rules.
- **The score recap is a special case.** A running score ("3 out of 5 so far") is
  related to the viewer's own performance, not to the question — and by the Keller
  logic it is incidental material in the dead zone. It should not appear after a reveal.
  If a score is shown, it belongs at the end of the round, as the payoff the hook
  promised (M2 §5), where it is the target of the final gap, not an interruption of
  one.

### (d) Retrieval-practice framing — the viewer answering, score-keeping, repeat visits

[INFERENCE from §5] The format's claim to be more than entertainment is that the
viewer *tries* before they are told. Three consequences:

- **The attempt is the product.** The video must be built so that answering in the
  head is the natural behaviour, not an optional extra: the question stays visible, the
  countdown gives time, and the voice asks the viewer to commit ("say it in your head
  before the timer ends"). A viewer who waits passively for the answer is restudying,
  and restudying is the control condition that loses (§5.3). The factory cannot force an
  attempt, but it can design for one — and comments ("I got 4/5") are the observable
  trace that attempts happened.
- **Score-keeping is a memory device, not just a game.** A viewer who tracks their own
  score is doing something the lab literature recognises: they are monitoring their own
  retrieval, which is the metacognitive step that makes the next retrieval attempt
  sharper. Score-keeping also creates the reason to return: a score is only meaningful
  against yesterday's score. This is D12's (gamification) material, but the D11 reason
  for it is memory, not engagement: the score is the viewer's record of what they
  retrieved, and the record is what makes the next attempt a *repeated* retrieval rather
  than a first one.
- **Repeat visits are spaced retrieval.** Karpicke & Roediger (2008) show that the
  benefit is in *repeated* retrieval after success, and that dropping an item after one
  success costs two-thirds of the recall a week later. Applied across videos, this means
  the factory should bring material back: a question from last week returns in a new
  form, a topic recurs at a higher difficulty, a "you got this wrong last Tuesday"
  callback. A channel whose videos never revisit anything is a channel whose viewers
  forget everything within a week — however good each single video is. The returning
  question is also the cheapest possible content: the question bank already contains it.

### (e) Hypotheses for the experiment log

Four hypotheses, each with a falsifier, ready to enter the experiment log. All use the
existing protocol (one variable, 7 days + 1,000 views minimum, win only on comments/view
AND completion together) unless stated otherwise.

- **H-M3-1 — Gap size (common-mistake vs straightforward).** A round built around
  common-mistake questions (obvious answer wrong) earns higher comments/view and higher
  completion than a difficulty-matched round of straightforward questions, because the
  violated expectation drives both commenting and staying for the reveal.
  *Falsifier:* if the common-mistake round's comments/view is not higher than the
  control's, the hypercorrection mechanism does not transfer to the feed in this form;
  drop the slot from "at least one per round" to "occasional".
- **H-M3-2 — Post-reveal placement (the rule-6 test).** A CTA placed immediately after
  a reveal earns fewer follows per view (and no more comments/view) than the same CTA
  placed at the end of the video after the final payoff; and a fact placed after a reveal
  is recalled less often in comments (fewer viewers repeating it) than the same fact
  placed before the first question. *Falsifier:* if the post-reveal CTA performs as well
  as the end CTA, the Keller transfer fails and rule 6 is demoted to a preference; if the
  post-reveal fact is repeated as often, the dead zone does not apply to this format.
  *Note:* this test needs a CTA whose effect can be attributed per placement, and a
  comment-coding step (count viewers repeating the fact) — it is the most laborious of
  the four and should run after the format is stable.
- **H-M3-3 — The "why" line.** A reveal that includes a one-sentence "why the answer
  is true" earns higher comments/view than the same reveal with the bare answer only,
  because post-answer interest (Fastrich) is higher when the answer is interesting.
  *Falsifier:* if comments/view does not rise, the "why" is cut as dead weight and the
  reveal is kept to the bare answer; the extra seconds go to think time instead.
- **H-M3-4 — The returning question.** A video that revisits one question from the
  previous week (same fact, new wording) earns higher comments/view than a video of all
  new questions, because returning viewers recognise the material and comment their
  improved score, and because repeated retrieval is the mechanism that makes the first
  encounter stick. *Falsifier:* if comments/view is not higher, and no viewer comments
  on the return, the revisit is dropped; the channel treats each video as standalone.
  *Note:* this test can only run once the account has a second week of videos.

## 10. What this changes in the factory

### Rules adopted now (academic evidence, or labelled inference)

1. **Gap sizing: the "almost know" rule.** Every question must pass the foothold test
   (something the viewer already knows) and the guess test (a viewer can form a guess
   in the think time). Difficulty rises across a round; the hardest question is last.
   (Loewenstein 1994; Kang et al. 2009; Metcalfe 2020; [INFERENCE] for the operational
   tests.)
2. **The reveal has three jobs.** State the answer unambiguously; show why the likely
   guess was wrong in one sentence; give the one fact that makes the true answer feel
   inevitable. Then a clean 1–2 second beat on the answer before the next hook.
   (Fastrich et al. 2018; Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006; [INFERENCE] for the 1–2 s beat.)
3. **M2 rule 6 is confirmed and refined (see §8).** Nothing unrelated that must be
   remembered or acted on immediately after a reveal — no fact, CTA, or branding. The
   next beat is the next question's hook. A one-sentence "why" belonging to the answer
   just given is part of the reveal, not a violation. (Keller et al. 2024; Fastrich et
   al. 2018.)
4. **At least one common-mistake question per round.** The obvious answer is wrong.
   This is the format's strongest memory device and its most commentable question type.
   (Kang et al. 2009; Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006; Fastrich et al. 2018.)
5. **Fact-checking is memory-critical, not just policy-critical.** A wrong answer is
   hyper-encoded by exactly the viewers who engaged hardest (Metcalfe 2019). Every
   answer is verified before rendering; "obvious" answers get the same verification as
   obscure ones. (Metcalfe 2019; links D11 to D9.)
6. **No score recap after a reveal.** A running score is incidental material in the
   dead zone. Scores appear at the end of the round, as the payoff. (Keller et al. 2024;
   [INFERENCE] for the score application.)

### Hypotheses queued for testing

- H-M3-1 (gap size / common-mistake), H-M3-2 (post-reveal placement), H-M3-3 (the
  "why" line), H-M3-4 (the returning question) — §9(e). H-M3-2 is the direct test of
  the refined rule 6; H-M3-1 and H-M3-3 can run as soon as the format is stable;
  H-M3-4 needs a second week of videos.

### Changes to EXP-001 / EXP-002

- **None.** The v2 prototypes are unchanged: they are the control comparison, and
  changing question design now would mix the D11 variables into the trivia-vs-riddles
  comparison. Their questions were not designed against the "almost know" rule — that
  is information about the baseline, not a reason to rebuild it mid-test.
- The comparison's interpretation changes: if completion is low, the second suspect
  (after the opening, M2) is question sizing — questions with no foothold, or reveals
  that do not do the three jobs. The question-design round (H-M3-1, H-M3-3) is the next
  experiment after the verdict, using the winner format.

### Capabilities created

- **Question-design rules** (§9a–b, rules 1–2 above) — D11's "becomes a capability" is
  met: gap size, reveal timing, and post-reveal placement are now specified as rules
  with named evidence, not as taste.
- **The post-reveal dead zone** (§3, §8) — a named, evidenced constraint on the
  factory's beat order, with a test (H-M3-2) that can overturn it.
- **The retrieval-practice framing** (§9d) — the reason the think time exists, the
  reason to revisit material, and the link from D11 to D12 (the return loop).

### Re-check triggers

- When the first Studio retention curves arrive: re-read §9(b) against real curves —
  if viewers leave *during* think time, the hold is too long whatever the lab says;
  if they leave *at* the reveal, the reveal is failing job (1).
- When H-M3-2 runs: if the post-reveal CTA performs as well as the end CTA, rule 6 is
  demoted and this module is corrected in writing, with the experiment cited.
- If a replication or extension of Keller et al. (2024) appears — especially one with a
  delayed test, an incentive to remember, or video material — re-check §3 and §8
  against it. The interference finding is one study, online, during COVID, with an
  immediate test; it is the best evidence available, not the last word.
- When M4 (gamification, D12) lands: re-check §9(d) against it — score-keeping and
  returning questions are shared territory, and the two modules must agree on the
  mechanism (memory vs engagement) each rule serves.

## 11. What we could not verify

1. **No optimal hold time.** No study found varies the gap-hold duration against a
   retention or memory outcome. Gruber used 14 seconds in a scanner; Keller used
   immediate presentation and a 4-second delay. The factory's think time is set by its
   own test (M2 H3), not by the literature.
2. **No feed transfer.** No study in this module measures swipe behaviour, completion,
   or following — all lab outcomes are memory tests. Every application in §9 is an
   inference from lab memory to feed behaviour, labelled as such.
3. **The Zeigarnik effect.** M2 invoked it as the usual name for an open loop holding
   attention. This module did not check its primary literature and does not use it as
   evidence. It remains a named hypothesis.
4. **Desirable-difficulty guidelines.** Bjork's framework is cited from the concept's
   standard description (Bjork 1994; Bjork & Bjork 2011); the three practical
   guidelines in §6 are reported from secondary summaries (Wikipedia; practitioner
   explainers), not read in the primary text. The framework is used as a lens, not as a
   source of numbers.
5. **Adesope feedback moderation.** The paper itself was read (abstract and results
   sections) and the g = 0.51 / g = 0.93 figures were verified in the paper text. The
   feedback moderation (g = 0.63 with feedback vs 0.60 without, no significant
   difference) is reported from a secondary summary of the paper, not verified in the
   paper text. It is consistent with Roediger & Karpicke (2006), which was read
   directly, and is not load-bearing.
6. **Keller et al. — no replication found.** The interference finding rests on a single
   2024 study (N = 239, online, COVID-era, immediate test, no trivia-memory test).
   No replication, extension, or contradiction was found in this module's searches.
   The finding is adopted because it is peer-reviewed, consistent across three task
   versions, and directly on point — but it is one study, and §10's re-check trigger
   names the conditions under which it would be revised.
7. **Curiosity across a round.** Whether curiosity accumulates, fatigues, or resets
   across 5–8 questions in one video is [UNPROVEN] — the lab asks about one question
   at a time. The rising-difficulty mix rule (§9a) is a design choice, not a finding.
